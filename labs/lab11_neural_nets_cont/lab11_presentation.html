1 Edge detectors and channels

In lab 10 we designed two edge detectors by hand and said that a CNN learns its kernels from the data. Here we look at what it learns.

1.1 A CNN with larger first-layer kernels

Consider a CNN like make_cnn() from the previous lab, with two changes: the first convolution has \(5 \times 5\) kernels instead of \(3 \times 3\), and every convolution has half as many channels (8, 16 and 32 instead of 16, 32 and 64). The diagram shows the shape of the data after each layer, for one image: \(C \times H \times W\) is \(C\) channels (feature maps) of \(H \times W\) values. Each value of a channel of the first convolution is computed from the \(5 \times 5\) pixels around it, like the red square; the same kernel slides over every position. The argument first_scale of make_cnn5 multiplies the initial weights of the first convolution (and sets its biases to zero); we use it below.

def make_cnn5(first_scale=1.0):
    model = nn.Sequential(
        nn.Conv2d(1, 8, kernel_size=5, padding=2), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),      # 1 x 28 x 28 -> 8 x 14 x 14
        nn.Conv2d(8, 16, kernel_size=3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),     # -> 16 x 7 x 7
        nn.Conv2d(16, 32, kernel_size=3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),    # -> 32 x 3 x 3
        nn.Flatten(),                                                               # -> 32 * 3 * 3 = 288
        nn.Linear(32 * 3 * 3, 64), nn.ReLU(),
        nn.Linear(64, 10),
    )
    if first_scale != 1.0:
        with torch.no_grad():
            model[0].weight *= first_scale
            model[0].bias.zero_()
    return model

1.2 Training

We now train the CNN with two modifications relative to the training of cnn_default:

  • the initial weights of the first convolution are multiplied by 0.05, so that what training adds dominates;
  • the training images get random pixel noise (for each minibatch, a standard deviation drawn uniformly between 0 and 0.5). Section 3 comes back to this noise augmentation.

The goal is to make sure the trained kernels are robust, and are indeed learned, as opposed to being merely inherited from a large random initialization.

train adds the noise to the validation images too, so the validation accuracies printed below are on noisy images, while the test accuracy is on clean ones.

def kernel_similarity(W_a, W_b):
    """Absolute cosine similarity between matching kernels of two weights of shape (channels, 1, k, k)."""
    return F.cosine_similarity(W_a.flatten(1), W_b.flatten(1)).abs().numpy()


def roughness(W):
    """Mean squared difference between neighboring kernel pixels over twice the mean square of the kernel:
    about 1 for independent noise, close to 0 for a smooth kernel."""
    W = W[:, 0]
    d = torch.cat([(W[:, 1:] - W[:, :-1]).flatten(1), (W[:, :, 1:] - W[:, :, :-1]).flatten(1)], dim=1)
    return ((d ** 2).mean(dim=1) / (2 * (W ** 2).mean(dim=(1, 2)))).numpy()


def add_noise(images, sd):
    """Add independent normal noise with standard deviation sd to every pixel."""
    return images + sd * torch.randn_like(images)


def random_noise(images):
    return add_noise(images, 0.5 * torch.rand(1).item())

Three kinds of channels can be read off the kernels, with the help of the two printed lines: a kernel whose sum is close to 0 compared with the sum of its absolute values takes differences, and one where the ratio is close to 1 takes an average.

  • Edge detectors (5, 3 and 0; ratios \(-0.14\), \(0.23\) and \(0.18\)). Channel 5 is the cleanest: positive in the top rows and negative in the bottom rows. This is the horizontal edge detector of lab 10 with the sign flipped: that one was negative on top and positive below, so it was positive where the ink is below an edge, and channel 5 is positive where the ink is above one. On the 7 it keeps the horizontal bar and nothing of the long stroke; on the 0, the top and the bottom of the ring. Channels 0 and 3 have their positive and negative parts side by side, and keep the strokes that are close to vertical.
  • Ink detectors (1 and 7; ratios 1.00 and 0.98). Their kernels are positive everywhere: they blur the image, like the blur of lab 10.
  • In between (2, 4 and 6; ratios 0.53, 0.72 and 0.50). Mostly positive, but with the weight on one side or along one direction.

The background of channels 0, 3 and 5 is gray instead of white because their biases are positive (+0.06, +0.05 and +0.11): these channels are slightly on where there is no ink. The bias of channel 6 is positive too, but too small (+0.03) to show.

Here an edge detector is meant in the image-processing sense: a kernel that matches a patch that is dark on one side of a line and light on the other (a step edge), like the edge detectors of lab 10. Its positive half wants ink and its negative half wants none, so it also tells on which side of the edge the ink is.

1.3 Tuning curves

To measure what a channel prefers, we show the network a synthetic image that is exactly the pattern of an edge detector: a half-plane, with ink on one side of a straight line through the center and none on the other. We call \(\theta\) the direction in which the ink side lies, counterclockwise from the right: at \(0°\) the right half of the image has ink, at \(90°\) the top half. We record the value of each channel at the center of the image, on the boundary. Doing this for \(\theta\) from \(0°\) to \(360°\) gives the tuning curve of the channel. Going around the full circle matters: the half-planes at \(\theta\) and \(\theta + 180°\) have their boundary in the same direction but the ink on opposite sides.

def half_plane(theta_deg, width=0.5):
    """A 28 x 28 image with ink on one side of a straight line through the center. theta is the direction,
    counterclockwise from the right, in which the ink side lies (0: right half, 90: top half). The boundary is
    a logistic step: the ink goes from 10% to 90% over about 4.4 * width pixels."""
    theta = np.deg2rad(theta_deg)
    r, c = np.mgrid[0:28, 0:28] - 13.5
    x, y = c, -r                                           # y points up
    s = x * np.cos(theta) + y * np.sin(theta)              # signed distance to the line, positive on the ink side
    return torch.tensor(1 / (1 + np.exp(-s / width)), dtype=torch.float32)[None]

1.4 Channels: what the deeper layers see

The first convolution takes 1 channel (the image) to 8. The second takes those 8 channels to 16, and its weight has four dimensions:

for i in (0, 3, 6):
    conv = cnn[i]
    c_out, c_in, k, _ = conv.weight.shape
    print(f"layer {i}: weight shape {tuple(conv.weight.shape)} = (out channels, in channels, height, width),  "
          f"each output adds up N = {k} x {k} x {c_in} = {k * k * c_in} inputs")
layer 0: weight shape (8, 1, 5, 5) = (out channels, in channels, height, width),  each output adds up N = 5 x 5 x 1 = 25 inputs
layer 3: weight shape (16, 8, 3, 3) = (out channels, in channels, height, width),  each output adds up N = 3 x 3 x 8 = 72 inputs
layer 6: weight shape (32, 16, 3, 3) = (out channels, in channels, height, width),  each output adds up N = 3 x 3 x 16 = 144 inputs

Each output channel of the second convolution has one \(3 \times 3\) kernel per input channel, and adds the 8 results: it looks for a combination of first-layer features in a small neighborhood. An output pixel is therefore a weighted sum of \(N = K^2 C_{\text{in}}\) inputs (this \(N\) comes back in Section 2).

The idea of these channels is to assemble information from the edge detectors to detect more complicated structures such as large regions forming an edge with the same orientation, or compositions of edges forming more complicated structures.

Consider the two examples next showing channels 0 and 10 from the second convolutional layer.

  • Channel 0: detects a larger edge on the right from smaller ones.
  • Channel 10: detects two separate edges one on the left and one on the right (which is something not detectable by any individual kernel from the first convolutional layer).

Channel 0 is able to detect the large edge in two different ways: one by just checking whether the edge detectors with the same target patterns are strongly activated on one side of the region, and the other by building an edge detector itself to operate on the channels which just detect whether ink is present in the region or not.

Channel 10 on the other hand, checks for an edge on the right of the region, for example by looking at channel 4, and one edge on the left, for example by looking at channel 3. If both of these are found (together with similar patterns captured by other edges), the channel is high on the region, indicating a composition of one edge on the left and one on the right separated by a white space.

Note that the presence of a shape like this is strongly indicative of the image being a 0 or 4, and certainly not a 1 or a 7 for example.

This is exactly the type of “features” one would like to have to classify handwriting in general. What is striking is that the neural network learns these on its own, without requiring a human to manually engineer those features.

Receptive fields. Each value of a feature map depends only on a square patch of the input image, its receptive field. After the first convolution, the value at position \((i, j)\) depends on the \(5 \times 5\) pixels around pixel \((i, j)\) (the red square in the diagram at the top). The patches that activate a channel the most. For one channel of one convolution, we:

  1. pass the 2,000 test images through the network up to that convolution and its ReLU (before the pooling), which gives one feature map of the channel per image;
  2. find, in each image, the position \((i, j)\) where the channel is largest;
  3. rank the images by this largest value, and keep the first 8;
  4. cut out of each of these 8 images the receptive field of its position \((i, j)\).

top_patches does these four steps for every channel at once. The arguments step, offset and size describe the receptive fields of the table: the value at position \((i, j)\) depends on the pixels in rows \(\text{step} \cdot i - \text{offset}\) to \(\text{step} \cdot i - \text{offset} + \text{size} - 1\), and likewise for the columns. For convolution 2, for example, position \((i, j)\) sees rows \(2i - 4\) to \(2i + 5\).

def top_patches(model, n_layers, images, step, offset, size, n_top=8):
    """For each channel of model[:n_layers], the n_top input patches with the largest value (one per image).
    A value at position (i, j) depends on the input rows step * i - offset, ..., step * i - offset + size - 1,
    and likewise for the columns. Returns an array of shape (channels, n_top, size, size)."""
    with torch.no_grad():
        acts = model[:n_layers](images)                          # (n, channels, h, w)
    n, C, h, w = acts.shape
    best, where = acts.reshape(n, C, -1).max(dim=2)              # largest value of each channel in each image
    pad = 8
    padded = F.pad(images, (pad, pad, pad, pad))                 # so that patches at the border fit
    out = torch.zeros(C, n_top, size, size)
    for ch in range(C):
        for t, img in enumerate(best[:, ch].argsort(descending=True)[:n_top]):
            i, j = divmod(where[img, ch].item(), w)
            top, left = step * i - offset + pad, step * j - offset + pad
            out[ch, t] = padded[img, 0, top:top + size, left:left + size]
    return out

2 Initialization in a deep CNN

2.1 What the lecture predicts

Before training, the weights are random. In lecture we saw what this does to a deep ReLU network. Take a layer whose outputs each add up \(N\) inputs, with independent weights of mean zero and variance \(\sigma^2\). If the preactivations of the previous layer have variance \(s^2\), those of this layer have variance about \[ \frac{N \sigma^2}{2} \, s^2 . \] So every layer multiplies the variance by \(N \sigma^2 / 2\). The same factor (with the number of outputs in place of \(N\)) applies to the gradients on the way back. After \(L\) layers the variance has changed by \((N \sigma^2 / 2)^L\), and the standard deviation by the square root of that, which explodes or vanishes unless \(N \sigma^2 / 2 = 1\). He initialization takes \(\sigma^2 = 2 / N\) (He et al. 2015; Prince 2023, sec. 7.5).

For a convolution, \(N = K^2 C_{\text{in}}\), as we saw above. We write the variance of the weights as \[ \sigma^2 = \frac{c}{N}, \] so that the predicted factor per layer is \(c / 2\), and He initialization is \(c = 2\).

2.2 A deep CNN

The CNN of section 1 has only three convolutions, so a bad factor is applied three times. To see the effect we use a deeper network: 20 convolutions (\(3 \times 3\), 16 channels, each followed by a ReLU) with no pooling between them, then the average of each channel over the image and one fully connected layer. To make it fast, the images are first reduced to \(14 \times 14\) by averaging \(2 \times 2\) blocks.

def make_deep_cnn(c=None, depth=20, width=16):
    """A plain deep CNN on 14 x 14 images. With c=None the weights keep PyTorch's default initialization.
    Otherwise the weights of each convolution are normal with variance c / N, N = 9 * (input channels),
    and its biases are zero."""
    layers, c_in = [nn.AvgPool2d(2)], 1                          # 1 x 28 x 28 -> 1 x 14 x 14
    for _ in range(depth):
        conv = nn.Conv2d(c_in, width, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
        if c is not None:
            nn.init.normal_(conv.weight, std=np.sqrt(c / (9 * c_in)))
            nn.init.zeros_(conv.bias)
        layers += [conv, nn.ReLU()]
        c_in = width
    layers += [nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1), nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(width, 10)]    # average each channel, then 10 logits
    return nn.Sequential(*layers)

2.3 The scale of each layer before training

We pass 256 training images through the network before any training, compute the loss and its gradient, and record three numbers for each convolution:

  • the standard deviation of its preactivations (its outputs, before the ReLU);
  • the standard deviation of its preactivations across images, at a fixed channel and position, averaged over channels and positions: how much the layer still depends on the input;
  • the standard deviation of the derivative of the loss with respect to its preactivations.

We do this for \(c = 0.5\), \(2\) (He) and \(8\), and for PyTorch’s default.

def layer_scales(model, xb, yb):
    """Pass a minibatch through the model and backpropagate the loss. For each convolution, return the standard
    deviation of (1) its preactivations, (2) its preactivations across images, (3) the gradient of the loss with
    respect to its preactivations."""
    preacts, h = [], xb
    for layer in model:
        h = layer(h)
        if isinstance(layer, nn.Conv2d):
            h.retain_grad()                      # keep d loss / d h after backward()
            preacts.append(h)
    F.cross_entropy(h, yb).backward()
    return (np.array([p.std().item() for p in preacts]),
            np.array([p.std(dim=0).mean().item() for p in preacts]),
            np.array([p.grad.std().item() for p in preacts]))

Zero padding at the borders lowers it by about 2% more.

The default weights have variance \(1 / (3N)\): this is \(c = 1/3\), six times smaller than He initialization. The predicted factor per layer is \(c / 2 = 1/6 \approx 0.17\), which is the backward factor we measured.

The default also draws the biases at random, uniformly between \(-1 / \sqrt{N}\) and \(1 / \sqrt{N}\), instead of setting them to zero. Each layer adds its biases to a signal that has almost vanished, so the preactivations keep a standard deviation of about 0.05 (left panel above), the size of the biases (\(1 / \sqrt{3N} \approx 0.05\) for \(N = 144\)), but they come from the biases and are the same for every image (middle panel).

This did not matter in lab 10. With three convolutions, a factor of this size is applied only three times, so the scale of the gradients changes by a factor of the order of 10 (for example \(\sqrt{6^3} \approx 15\)), not millions, and Adam adapts its steps to the scale of the gradients. (The exact factors differ there, because the number of channels doubles at each convolution and max pooling changes the scale.) It matters for deep networks.

  • Noise augmentation works. At a noise standard deviation of 0.5, the accuracy goes from 68.1% to 93.6%, and at 1, twice the largest noise seen in training, from 25.0% to 68.2%. The cost on clean images is small (98.0% to 97.6%).
  • Robustness is specific to the perturbation. Shift augmentation gives 95.8% on images shifted by 3 pixels, against 84.3% without it, but it does not help against noise. It gives up a little on each perturbation, more at the largest noise (63.0% against 68.2% at standard deviation 1), and a little on clean images (96.7%, the lowest of the four): with the same budget it has more to learn.

Augmentation teaches the network the perturbations it is shown, and nothing says that it will be robust to the others. If we do not know which perturbations the test images will have, we have to guess them, and showing several at once is cheap here: one network handles both, at a small cost on each.

The perturbations so far were ones we chose. def digit_height(images):
    """Number of rows from the first to the last row with ink (a pixel above 0.5), for each image."""
    ink_rows = (images[:, 0] > 0.5).any(dim=2).float()          # (n, 28): 1 if the row has ink
    first = ink_rows.argmax(dim=1)
    last = 27 - ink_rows.flip(1).argmax(dim=1)
    return (last - first + 1).numpy()

MNIST digits are 20 pixels tall: they were scaled to fit in a \(20 \times 20\) box and centered in the \(28 \times 28\) image (LeCun et al. 1998). The perturbation to be robust to is a change of scale. We train one more copy of cnn_default, on training images zoomed by a random factor between 0.7 and 1.5, a range that includes 1.4. random_scale draws a factor for each image and resamples the image with F.affine_grid and F.grid_sample.

def random_scale(images, low=0.7, high=1.5):
    """Zoom each image about its center by its own random factor between low and high (zeros outside the image)."""
    s = torch.empty(len(images)).uniform_(low, high)
    theta = torch.zeros(len(images), 2, 3)
    theta[:, 0, 0] = theta[:, 1, 1] = 1 / s           # the output at position p (from the center) is the input at p / s
    grid = F.affine_grid(theta, images.shape, align_corners=False)
    return F.grid_sample(images, grid, align_corners=False)

With this preprocessing cnn_default gets about 96% of them right (not shown).

Edge detectors, channels and receptive fields. Orientation tuning and receptive fields come from neuroscience: Hubel and Wiesel (1962) recorded single neurons in the visual cortex of cats and found cells that respond to an edge or a bar at one orientation, in one region of the visual field. The first layer of a CNN trained on natural images learns kernels of the same kind, edge detectors at many orientations and color blobs (Krizhevsky et al. 2012, fig. 3). Looking at the input patches that make a channel the largest, as we did for the three convolutions, is the approach of Zeiler and Fergus (2014), who found the same progression from edges to parts to objects in a large network. Olah et al. (2017) survey this and other ways to see what a channel detects, including synthesizing the input that a channel responds to the most. For CNNs in general, see Prince (2023, chap. 10) and LeCun et al. (1998).

Initialization. Glorot and Bengio (2010) studied how the scale of the activations and of the gradients changes across the layers of a deep network, and proposed to choose the variance of the weights to keep it constant. He et al. (2015) adapted the argument to ReLU networks, which gives the factor \(c / 2\) per layer of section 2. See also Prince (2023, sec. 7.5).

Robustness and augmentation. Geirhos et al. (2018) trained networks on images with one kind of distortion, such as additive noise, and found that they became robust to that distortion and generally not to the others, as in our comparison of noise and shift augmentation. Azulay and Weiss (2019) show that even large CNNs can change their prediction when the image is shifted by one pixel, although the convolutions alone are equivariant to shifts. Shorten and Khoshgoftaar (2019) survey the data augmentation methods used for images.
