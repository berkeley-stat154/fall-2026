Lab 5 Exercise - Cross Validation (cont.)

Author

Josh Davis/Lucas Schwengber

Learning goals

  1. Understanding what happens with model performance estimates when data is non-i.i.d..
  2. Providing reasonable estimators of performance in such cases.
  3. Performing hyperparameter selection and inference on such setting.

1 Challenge

We provide historical data from EIA energy consumption. Your goal is to select a set of optimal hyperparameters, and estimate its performance on predicting consumption (given the same covariates) over a continuous stretch of hourly data from this week, from Thursday 10/15/2026 at 9 AM until Saturday 10/17/2026 at 9 PM (California time).

The data covers hourly electricity demand in the California ISO (CAISO) balancing area, together with weather at two locations inside it:

  • X_train.csv
  • y_train.csv

1.1 The training data

Both files have one row per hour, in the same order, from 2023-01-01 to 2026-10-10 (32,966 rows).

Response (y_train.csv). demand_mw is the hourly electricity demand of the CAISO balancing area, in megawatts. It comes from the EIA-930 Hourly Electric Grid Monitor, where each grid operator reports its demand to the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

Features (X_train.csv).

  • timestamp_utc, timestamp_local: the start of the hour, in UTC and in California time. These are only to help you split the data, and must not be used as features.
  • Calendar: hour, day_of_week (0 = Monday), day_of_year, is_holiday (U.S. federal holidays) and day_index (number of days since 2023-01-01).
  • Weather, for each of two stations, Fresno (fresno_*) and Riverside (riverside_*), chosen to represent the northern and southern parts of the state: temp (temperature in °C), rh (relative humidity, %), rad (shortwave solar radiation in W/m²), and tmax_24h/tmin_24h (the highest and lowest temperature over the 24 hours ending at that row).

How it was obtained. The demand was downloaded from the six-month bulk files that EIA publishes on its website, which are updated with about a one-day lag. The weather was downloaded from Open-Meteo (its historical forecast API), which serves hourly values from a weather model at the given coordinates. The two sources were joined by timestamp, and the features above were computed from them. Hours with missing demand (about 130, mostly around daylight saving changes) and one obvious reporting glitch were removed.

1.2 How it relates to the evaluation data

The evaluation data is built with exactly the same process, the same balancing area, the same two weather stations, the same columns and the same day_index origin. The only difference is the time period: the rows are a continuous series from Thursday 10/15/2026 at 9 AM until Saturday 10/17/2026 at 9 PM (California time), which come after the last training row. They did not exist when the training data was downloaded, and the EIA only publishes the demand for those days after they have happened, so the evaluation data is genuinely unseen: it is in the future with respect to everything you have, rather than a random subset of the same period. That is why a model’s error on the training period, as estimated by a standard shuffled cross-validation, is not guaranteed to reflect its error on this new data.

1.3 Allowed methods

You may only use lgb.LGBMRegressor, with any hyperparameters you like, trained only on X_train and y_train. Your final model should not use the time slot columns. They are just to assist your data splitting (see below).

2 How to submit your assignment

You will submit a JSON file containing a dictionary with the hyperparameters you optimized and your estimate for its performance on the future data described above. Your estimate of the error should be as the RMSE for the fraction of mean consumption in the training data (like in the presentation).

You should also submit your code.

You can save the JSON file like this:

import json

hparams_info = {
    "min_child_samples": 20,
    "learning_rate": 0.1,
    "n_estimators": 100,
    "error_estimate": 0.1412,
    "error_estimate_sd": 0.0552
}

output_fn = f"hparams_info_{codename}.json"
with open(f"{path}/{output_fn}", "w") as f:
    f.write(json.dumps(hparams_info))

You can add as many hyperparameters as you like, but they should be named exactly as they are in the LGBMRegressor constructor. Your estimate of the error should be named "error_estimate" and your estimate of its standard deviation should be named "error_estimate_sd".

The name of the json file should be "hparams_info_{codename}.json" where {codename} is a secret codename of your group’s choosing, that you will be able to use later to identify your submission in the leaderboard. The file, alongside your code, should be submitted to GradeScope by Tuesday at 9 PM.

3 How your hyperparameters will be scored

We will train a lightgbm model on the training data provided using the hyperparameters you submitted, then evaluate the model on a held-out set comprising data with the same features (except the time slot columns) but collected as a continuous series from Thursday 10/15/2026 at 9 AM until Saturday 10/17/2026 at 9 PM (California time).

We will run this code:

import lightgbm as lgb
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import json

with open(f"{path}/hparams_info_{codename}.json") as f:
    hparams_info = json.load(f)
params = {k: v for k, v in hparams_info.items() if not k.startswith("error_estimate")}

feature_cols = ["hour", "day_of_week", "day_of_year", "is_holiday", "day_index",
                "fresno_temp", "fresno_rh", "fresno_rad", "fresno_tmax_24h", "fresno_tmin_24h",
                "riverside_temp", "riverside_rh", "riverside_rad", "riverside_tmax_24h", "riverside_tmin_24h"]

X = pd.read_csv(f"{path}/X_train.csv")[feature_cols]
y = pd.read_csv(f"{path}/y_train.csv").squeeze()
X_holdout = pd.read_csv(f"{path}/X_holdout.csv")[feature_cols]
y_holdout = pd.read_csv(f"{path}/y_holdout.csv").squeeze()

# demand as a fraction of mean consumption in the training data
mean_demand = y.mean()

model = lgb.LGBMRegressor(**params)
model.fit(X, y / mean_demand)
pred = model.predict(X_holdout)
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((y_holdout / mean_demand - pred) ** 2))

Your model should only use the 15 feature columns listed above; in particular, it should not use the timestamp_utc and timestamp_local columns. As you can see, I’m passing your hyperparameters to LGBMRegressor’s constructor. You are free to optimize any and all of the hyperparameters that the constructor accepts. Any hyperparameter you do not include in the JSON file is left at LGBMRegressor’s default value.

A leaderboard will be published with every group’s codename alongside its loss on the holdout set together with the estimated error. The rank will be based on both the loss and the estimated error. No other identifying information will be shared, so no other student will be able know your rank on the leaderboard. The leaderboard is for your own benefit!

LLM usage policy

You may use auto-completion for filling out small code snippets (affect around 2-3 lines of code) and documenting your code. Small code snippets are things like “add a label for the axis of the plot”, “change the color of the points”, “add an argument for the number of interactions in the fit”. These are small code operations which do not involve significant planning nor automated changes to the logic of the code without your supervision. All edits must be verified and accepted manually, and you should make sure you know what the code is doing.

Things that are explicitly not allowed are major edits to the code (affect > 10 lines) and ones which involve automating huge parts of the development of the logic structure of your analysis. For example, “write a function to extract features from the data”, or “run a test set validation loop and report the best model”.

Delegate to language models only things which you know exactly how you would have done it yourself or would be comfortable googling/asking for help from someone who already took the course/knows how to code, but who cannot do the learning for you.
